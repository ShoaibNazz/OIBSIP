1928 – 2016

Abdul Sattar Edhi

He answered the calls that no one else would answer.

Abdul Sattar Edhi was a Pakistani humanitarian who began with a single dispensary in Karachi and built one of the country’s largest welfare networks: ambulances, shelters, clinics and orphanages that served people of every faith and background. He lived simply, took no salary, and relied on public donations. This page is a short, respectful tribute to his life.

His life

How to read this page: the biography, timeline and legacy sections summarise facts from the sources listed at the end, in our own words. Anything labelled Author’s tribute is a personal reflection, not a historical claim.

Abdul Sattar Edhi was born in 1928 in Bantva, in Gujarat, then part of British India, into a Memon Muslim family. His mother encouraged him to look after others, and as a boy he ran errands for a neighbourhood dispensary, learning to notice the people in his village who needed help most.

After the Partition of India in 1947 his family moved to the newly formed Pakistan and settled in Karachi. A few years later, in 1951, he used his own savings to buy a small shop in the Mithadar district and opened a dispensary there. It was open to everyone, and its medicines were sold below market price.

His work grew quickly during the influenza epidemic of 1957. Donations arrived, and he bought an old van that became his first ambulance. He went on to add a maternity unit and nurse-training courses, then more centres and vehicles. The effort was funded by ordinary people’s donations, and Edhi preferred not to depend on government money.

In 1965 he married Bilquis, a nurse at the dispensary, and she became a full partner in the work. She ran the maternity home and organised the adoption of abandoned babies. Edhi himself was known for a very plain way of life: he reportedly owned only two sets of clothes and never drew a salary from his own organisation.

He died in Karachi on 8 July 2016 at the age of 88. He was given a state funeral and buried at Edhi Village on the outskirts of the city. According to reports, his corneas were donated, in line with his wish to help others even after his death.

Legacy

The Edhi Foundation is funded by private donations and serves people regardless of religion or ethnicity. These are the main areas of its work.

  • Emergency response

    The foundation runs one of the world’s largest volunteer ambulance networks, with 24-hour service. Reports put the fleet at 1,500 or more vehicles, and some accounts say about 1,800.

  • Children and families

    Cradles placed outside Edhi centres let families leave babies safely. By the time of his death Edhi was the registered parent or guardian of nearly 20,000 adopted children.

  • Shelter and care

    Its centres include orphanages, nursing homes, clinics, women’s shelters, kitchens and rehabilitation homes, with several hundred welfare centres across Pakistan.

  • Dignity for the dead

    The foundation built a burial service for unclaimed bodies, arranging funerals in line with each person’s own faith.

  • Help beyond Pakistan

    Relief operations have reached other regions of the world. After Hurricane Katrina in 2005, the foundation donated US$100,000 to relief efforts.

  • Independence

    Edhi refused government support for his work, arguing that the money belonged to the public. The foundation continues today under his family, with his son Faisal Edhi as chairman.

Author’s tribute

What stays with me is how ordinary his method was: he showed up, he asked no one’s creed before helping, and he kept doing it for decades. His life is a reminder that compassion works best as a daily habit, and that one person’s steady effort can grow into something that carries a whole country’s generosity.
